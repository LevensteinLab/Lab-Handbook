### PhD Students

**You apply to a program, not to my lab.** I can currently take PhD students through three Yale programs:

- [Interdepartmental Neuroscience Program (INP)](https://medicine.yale.edu/inp/)
- [Biochemistry, Quantitative Biology, Biophysics, and Structural Biology (BQBS)](https://medicine.yale.edu/bbs/tracks/biochemistry-quantitative-biophysics-structural-biology/)
- [Computational Biology and Biomedical Informatics (CBB)](https://cbb.yale.edu/)

All three are rotation programs. You do 2-4 rotations of about 3 months each in your first year, and then choose the lab where you'll do your PhD. The benefit is that you get broad training (including computational and theoretical neuroscience, which you can focus on in your electives) and real experience with a few potential mentors before committing to one. I'd encourage you to also look at other groups doing computational neuroscience and neuroAI at Yale, e.g. Shreya Saxena, Monika Jadi, and Kazuki Irie. Each program has its own requirements and deadlines, so pick the one whose coursework and community fit you best.

The slight downside of rotation programs is that I can't take students directly into my group, and individual faculty have very little influence over who gets accepted. Admissions decisions are made by people who don't have a stake in recruiting students to their own lab, and then you settle on an advisor and project over your first year. I think that's a good system, but it does change what's useful to talk about before you apply.

**Should you email me before applying?** You're welcome to, and a short email saying who you are, what in our work interests you, and which program you're considering is helpful. But please know that:

1. I have no influence over acceptance decisions, so a conversation now won't change your chances.
2. A lot will change between now and when you'd rotate, so I don't think it's very valuable to plan specific projects yet. If the directions you mention sound like a good fit, I'll likely be happy to develop them with you as a rotation project.

I get many requests to chat as application deadlines approach, and because the same conversation can happen once you're officially interviewing, I've had to decide to save most of these for the interview stage. Please don't read that as low enthusiasm about you or your application. It's a practical decision to protect the time I have for research and for mentoring the people already in the lab. That being said, if you're genuinely on the fence about whether to apply and there's specific information I could give you that would help you decide, let me know and I'll do my best to make time for a quick chat.

### Postdocs

**Fellowships.** These are the most realistic route, and also good for your career: having your own funding gives you independence and is one of the things faculty search committees look for. Some options:

- **[Wu Tsai Institute Postdoctoral Fellowship](PLACEHOLDER: link).** WTI fellows are postdocs of the institute rather than of a single lab: WTI pays the salary, and you have more independence and a higher salary than a typical postdoc. The fellowship is designed for either co-mentorship across two WTI labs or an open-ended interdisciplinary project, so it is *not* a mechanism for joining just my lab. If you're interested, find a co-mentor whose work bridges naturally with ours, and reach out to both of us *before* you apply. You write the proposal; your mentors confirm they're willing to supervise. The deadline has been October 1. [PLACEHOLDER: confirm for the current cycle.]
- **External fellowships**, e.g. NIH F32, NSF PRFB, HFSP, EMBO, Marie Curie, LSRF, Helen Hay Whitney, Jane Coffin Childs, and fellowships from your home country for researchers going abroad. See our [Fellowships/Grants](Resources/funding.md) page for a running list. If you plan to apply for one of these with me as sponsor, please get in touch well in advance: Yale needs the complete package about a week before the sponsor deadline, and I'll want time to give you real feedback.

**Think about the angle.** A postdoc is short, and it's not "PhD part two." It's where you build a research identity that's distinctively yours ("I did X in my PhD, Y in my postdoc, and here's the research program only I can do"). Many postdocs come to the lab to add a complementary skill, and I'm very happy to have people from experimental backgrounds. That being said, if you've never trained a neural network, a fully computational postdoc means retraining from scratch, and that's a hard place to be on a postdoc timeline. Often a better structure is to keep an experimental anchor and add computation at an angle, for example with an experimental primary mentor and me as a co-mentor. I'm happy to talk through which setup makes sense for you.

**What to send.** Your CV, a short paragraph on what you'd want to work on and how it connects to our work, and which funding route you have in mind.

### Applying to Grad School (Here or Anywhere)

Some general advice I find myself giving often:

- **Programs, not labs.** The same PI is often reachable through several programs with different mechanics (direct admit vs. rotations). Check which programs lead to the labs you care about. Admittedly, emailing every PI to ask is a lot of work for everyone, and I don't have a better answer yet.
- **Your statement of purpose is about your research trajectory, not your life story.** What you've done (and your specific role), the questions you want to pursue, and why this program. Name a few faculty with concrete overlaps. Explaining your reasoning matters more than whether a project "worked." Most are 1-2 pages. See e.g. [Berkeley](https://grad.berkeley.edu/admissions/application-process/writing-your-statements/) and [Cornell](https://gradschool.cornell.edu/inclusion/recruitment/prospective-students/writing-your-statement-of-purpose/) for good guides.
- **An academic CV is not a resume.** No bullet points describing what you did. Just list everything: positions, posters (with titles), talks, papers. Pro tip: add things as they happen, because it's painful to reconstruct later.
- **Make life easy for your letter writers.** At least one letter should come from a research mentor. Send them your CV, draft statement, a list of programs and deadlines, and a few bullets on what you did together. Remind them two weeks, one week, and two days before each deadline.
