So You Want to Join the Lab?

First, thanks for your interest. We're a neuroAI lab in the Wu Tsai Institute's Center for Neurocomputation and Machine Intelligence, broadly working on brain dynamics during sleep and how they support memory and navigation.

PhD Students

I can currently take PhD students through three graduate programs at Yale:

  • Interdepartmental Neuroscience Program (INP)
  • Biochemistry, Quantitative Biology, Biophysics, and Structural Biology (BQBS)
  • Computational Biology and Biomedical Informatics (CBB)

All three are rotation programs. You do 2-4 rotations of about 3 months each in your first year, and then choose the lab where you'll do your PhD. The benefit of a rotation program is that you get broad training (including computational and theoretical neuroscience, which you can focus on in your electives and through the Graduate Certificate in Brains, Minds, and Machines) and experience with a few potential mentors before committing to one. I'd encourage you to also look at other groups doing computational neuroscience and neuroAI at Yale, e.g. Shreya Saxena, Monika Jadi, and Kazuki Irie. Each program has its own requirements and deadlines, so pick the one whose coursework and community fit you best.

The slight downside of rotation programs is that I can't take students directly into my group, and individual faculty have very little influence over who gets accepted. Admissions decisions are made by people who don't have a stake in recruiting students to their own lab, and then you settle on an advisor and project over your first year. I think that's a good system, especially because it gives you the opportunity to pick a mentor who works well for you, but it does change what's useful to talk about before you apply.

Should you email me before applying? You're welcome to, and a short email saying who you are, what in our work interests you, and which program you're considering is helpful. But please know that:

  1. I have no influence over acceptance decisions, so a conversation now won't change your chances.
  2. A lot will change between now and when you'd rotate, so I don't think it's very valuable to discuss specific projects yet. If you're interested in the work the lab does, I'll likely be happy to develop a rotation project with you.

While I do very much enjoy the opportunity to chat with bright young scientists, I get many requests to chat as application deadlines approach, and because the same conversation can happen once you're officially interviewing, I've had to decide to save these for the interview stage. Please don't read that as low enthusiasm about you or your application. It's a practical decision to protect the time I have for research and for mentoring the people already in the lab. That being said, if you're genuinely on the fence about whether to apply and there's specific information I could give you that would help you decide, let me know and I'll do my best to make time for a quick chat.

Postdocs

NOTE: As of fall 2026 I don't have funding for another postdoc in my group, so the main route into the lab is through a fellowship.

Fellowships. These are the most realistic route, and also good for your career: having your own funding gives you independence and is one of the things faculty search committees look for. Some options:

  • Wu Tsai Institute Postdoctoral Fellowship. WTI fellows are postdocs of the institute rather than of a single lab: WTI pays the salary, and you have more independence and a higher salary than a typical postdoc. The fellowship is designed for either co-mentorship across two WTI labs or an open-ended interdisciplinary project, so it is not a mechanism for joining just my lab. If you're interested, find a co-mentor whose work bridges naturally with ours, and reach out to both of us before you apply.
  • External fellowships, e.g. NIH F32, NSF PRFB, HFSP, EMBO, Marie Curie, LSRF, Helen Hay Whitney, Jane Coffin Childs, and fellowships from your home country for researchers going abroad. See our Fellowships/Grants page for a running list. If you plan to apply for one of these with me as sponsor, please get in touch well in advance: Yale needs the complete package about a week before the sponsor deadline, and I'll want time to give you real feedback.

What to send. Your CV, a short paragraph on what you'd want to work on and how it connects to our work, and which funding route you have in mind.

Undergraduates

I'm happy to work with Yale undergraduates, and I take a limited number each year so that each person gets real mentorship. If you're interested, please reach out and I'll let you know if there's any projects in the lab that are looking for an undergraduate research assistant for which you'd be a good fit. Necessary background will vary with the project. However, programming experience (Python), linear algebra, and calculus are generally a good background experience to have. Bonus points if you've worked with pytorch before. You don't need to have trained a neural network before, but you should be excited to learn how.

What to send. A short email with your year, your coursework and any programming or research experience, what in our work interests you, and how many hours a week you could commit. Please attach a CV or transcript.

I'm not able to take undergraduate students from other institutations at this time, except through dedicated exchange/fellowship programs with Yale.

Contact

Email me at daniel.levenstein@yale.edu. I get a lot of email, so if I haven't responded in a couple of weeks, a polite follow-up is welcome.
